            text: 'In ancient mythology, the concept of a chimera was crafted around tales of natural abominations; the grotesque union of disparate forms. Yet in the present day, the chimera is no longer confined to our mythologies. Many places within our world have become crucibles of transmutation, resulting both from the aggression of time as well as the voracious consumption of human civilization. Animals born with too many limbs in polluted rivers, forests warped by industrial runoff, landscapes crafted in man’s image by extraction. From desolate radiation zones to the poisoned creeks of rural America, nature itself has become spliced, distorted, and reconfigured.'

                text: 'Alexander Rouse is an artist, designer, curator, and writer from South Carolina. His work was influenced from an early age by the natural world, religious iconography, and a deep curiosity for any art around him. After receiving a degree in graphic design, Alexander has pivoted to a more studio-focused practice, working on building stability as a fine artist and expanding his work to new dimensions. As a designer, he has received awards from the American Advertising Federation, and as an artist Alexander has exhibited in a number of galleries, received publication, and created client work across multiple applications.'

            type: paragraph

              -
                type: text
                text: 'Alexander bears a hereditary link to Southern culture and natural identity. His art is shaped by this history and the complex systems and personalities that come with it. In tandem with the agrarian lives of his ancestors, Alexander’s art takes a naturalistic focus, centering subjects of the natural world and objectifying those subjects in a narrative context. His work invites questions of selfhood, inheritance, and metanarrative, and cornerstone of his practice is a deep rooting in classical references, religious aesthetics, and semiotic materialism. His focus is water-based painting, with strong emphasis on illustration and draftsmanship. Additional mediums include cyanotype, assemblage, and pyrography. He takes special interest in combining familiar methodology with unusual materials—wood-burning into bone, cyanotype on wood, paintings as assemblage.'

                text: 'The Knighthood Series explore the brilliance of women in a society geared for men’s success. Historically suits of armor were not designed for women. Despite this, several female figures have donned a suit of armor made to a man’s measure. These female warriors achieved feats of great strength and courage without the advantage of consideration. Joan of Arc, who wore full plate armor as she led her forces during the Hundred Year’s War, is just one example of this. In this series, the knights don’t conquer outright with violence, but pursue great knowledge through the channels of nature. A strong light source represents the dawn of knowledge. These knights are understudies in a sense. They carry the burden of unfit armor, but persevere and conquer the expectations set upon them.'

                type: text
                text: "My drawing process relies on using traditional drawing materials to visually recreate digital manipulation of media. This method of working allows me to use the visual aesthetics of digital corruption as an analogy to document psychological experiences. Text rendered in a mix of readable script and pure pixelization creates narrative ambiguity, mimicking jpeg compression with graphite pencils in self portraits poses questions of identity and loss of self, and variations within the blending of charcoal powder creates forms where the line between pixel and skin is blurred. I find meaning in drawing as a tool of remembering and reflection. As I render text, the pixelization of letters reflects clarity of memory as well as privatization of vulnerable thoughts. Rendering portraits of my younger self provides me with a refreshed perspective of life at times where I find my memory lacking. I process the complex experience of online sexual predation I faced as an adolescent through a matured lens. The paper gives me a space to analyze the psychological states of both past and present selves through the meditative act of drawing.\_"
          -
            type: paragraph
            content:
              -
                type: text
                text: "Through the medium of collage I reconstruct, weather, and glitch digital and print images to challenge the viewer to consider how the trove of media they ingest each day affects the body, mind, and environment. As I scan dozens of social media videos with a handheld scanner, the result is a jarring, glitchy, abstracted image filled with snippets of text and bodies that reflect the nauseating and hazy feeling one often encounters after hours of media intake. In scanning myself behind a scratched and chipped acrylic screen, I create distortions of the body that are collaged together to represent the fractured and monstrous sense of self that can result as a by-product of the disconnect between the digital and physical bodies we simultaneously inhabit. When using print images, I transfer found A.I. generated content onto a physical substrate such as paper or canvas. The transformation of overly glossy and smooth digital renders into worn, decayed, and unrecognizable paper abstractions reflects the highly disposable and cheap quality nature of A.I. generated content. Through large scale and repetition, this work aims to bring a visceral portrayal of how “digital trash” can amount to environmental damage in the physical realm.\_"

                text: 'My current body of work is informed by my upbringing in the foothills of the Appalachian mountains where the organic and inorganic decay, infrastructure, and wildlife that pock-mark the landscape make their way into my compositions. Frequently, I employ animals or animal parts as totemic stand-ins for my lived experiences. These experiences often revolve around the rapid urbanization, socio-economic disparities, environmental concerns, and the underlying absurdities indicative of the region which are unearthed during the cathartic state I experience while drawing. I explore my subject matter through direct observation, photographic references, digital collage, and memory recall. This constant feedback loop of visual information as it passes from physical perception, the mind’s eye, and to my drawing hand allows me to transform my source material into new visual amalgams. This process allows for the unseen, absurd, or otherwise limbo states within my drawings to be born through material resonance and multiple lenses of observation.'
